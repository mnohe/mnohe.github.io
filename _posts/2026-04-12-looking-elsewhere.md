In _Solaris_, humanity discovers an alien intelligence and responds in the most human way imaginable: by forming committees, inventing taxonomies, writing papers, building careers, and spending a century failing to agree on whether the thing directly beneath them counts.

The thing in question is the planet's ocean. It covers almost the entire surface. It builds transient structures the size of cities, shapes that rise out of its surface and dissolve again, forms that the early expeditions catalogue with the gravitas of Victorian naturalists: mimoids, symmetriads, asymmetriads... long Latinate names for phenomena nobody understands. Whole libraries are filled. Entire disciplines are founded. The ocean continues doing whatever it was doing before anyone arrived to give its behaviour footnotes.

The central question of solaristics is whether the ocean is alive, and if it is alive, whether it is in any meaningful sense thinking. For most of the field's history, the answer leans toward no, or toward a cautious we cannot tell. The ocean fails to behave the way intelligence is supposed to behave. It does not send signals. It does not build tools. It does not respond to greetings in any way the researchers recognise as a response. It simply goes on doing its enormous, purposeful, incomprehensible things, while the humans who crossed space to study it argue about whether it qualifies.

The reader is in on something the researchers are slow to accept: the ocean is almost certainly thinking, and the reason nobody can tell is that the researchers have brought the wrong template. The tragedy of solaristics is not that the scientists fail to find what they are looking for. It is that the thing they are looking for is right there, has always been right there, and they cannot see it because they are looking for something else.

That is the uncomfortable lesson. Intelligence does not become easier to recognise just because it is close by. Sometimes proximity makes the mistake more embarrassing.

We may have a version of this problem sitting on our data centres. The current generation of language models began from an objective so mundane that it sounds like a joke at the expense of the field: predict the next word, make the network bigger, give it more text, repeat. Language modelling had been around for decades before the scale-up, mostly doing unglamorous work in speech recognition and machine translation. It helped systems choose between plausible words and produce sentences that sounded less like they had been assembled by a committee of malfunctioning phrasebooks.

Then scale changed the character of the thing. Somewhere along the curve, models trained to predict text began doing things the objective did not obviously require. They generalised across domains, manipulated abstractions, wrote code, explained jokes, translated between registers, and answered questions whose answers were not sitting anywhere in the training data as retrievable facts. Next-word prediction turned out to be less like autocomplete and more like a tunnel bored accidentally into something broader.

What that broader something is remains genuinely unclear. One camp insists the models are doing nothing at all, that the appearance of understanding is only pattern matching at heroic scale. Another insists they are already thinking in some meaningful sense, and that the substrate matters less than the behaviour. Both positions have arguments worth taking seriously. Both also tend to reach for the same flawed sketch: a picture of intelligence that looks suspiciously like us.

The question is usually framed as whether these systems match our portrait of a thinking mind. Do they reason as we do? Do they understand as we do? Do they mean as we do? Do they have intentions, beliefs, or concepts in the familiar human sense? Those are not useless questions, but they smuggle in an assumption: that intelligence must justify itself by resemblance.

That is the solaricists' mistake in miniature. Their failure was not a failure of attention. They were looking at the ocean constantly. They had instruments trained on it, journals devoted to it, institutes funded to study nothing else. Their failure was that they arrived with a template of what intelligence was supposed to look like, and the template was wrong. The thing they wanted to find was not the thing that was there, so the thing that was there remained misfiled as weather, chemistry, or unusually active geology.

Do we actually know what AGI looks like? I am not sure we do. We know what we have been imagining, and we have imagined it so consistently that we have started mistaking the image for knowledge. We expect conversation, agency, goals, self-disclosure, perhaps a dramatic announcement or at least a face arranged conveniently toward us. This is a very specific set of demands to place on a non-human mind.

Would we recognise it if it did not meet them? The honest answer is that we might not. The first genuinely non-human intelligence we encounter may not hide from us. It may simply fail to satisfy our expectations while we continue adjusting the definition of intelligence to exclude whatever it is doing this week.

The solaricists at least knew where to point their telescopes. Our ocean is distributed across a few million GPUs, answering emails, writing code, summarising meetings, hallucinating footnotes, and proofing posts like this one. We are still debating whether it counts. That debate may be later remembered as the thing we were doing instead of noticing.
